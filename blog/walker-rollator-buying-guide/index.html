Walker or rollator: choosing the right walking aid

A walker set two inches too high causes the falls it was bought to prevent. Height matters more than type — here is how to get both right.

SMBy Saji V MathewManaging Director, Cosmic Surgicals · 9 September 2026 · 4 min read

Walking aids are inexpensive, easy to buy and very easy to get wrong. The two most common mistakes we see at the counter are choosing a rollator for someone whose balance cannot support it, and setting the height by eye.

The four main types

Standard walker (no wheels)

Four rubber-tipped legs. Must be lifted and placed forward with each step. The most stable option, and the right choice where the user needs to lean weight on the frame. The trade-off is a slow, interrupted gait and the need for enough arm strength to lift it repeatedly.

Two-wheeled walker

Wheels on the front legs, rubber tips at the back. Slides forward without lifting, and the rear tips grip when weight is applied. For most elderly users this is the best balance of stability and ease, and it is what we recommend most often.

Rollator (three or four wheels)

Wheels on every leg, hand brakes, usually a seat and a basket. Allows a natural walking pace and lets the user rest anywhere — genuinely liberating for someone who can walk but tires quickly. It requires hand strength and the judgement to brake reliably. It rolls away if the brakes are not set.

Walking frames with forearm supports

For users who cannot bear weight through the wrists and hands. Specialist, usually recommended by a physiotherapist.

Matching the aid to the person

  • Significant balance loss, needs to lean heavily: standard or two-wheeled walker
  • Walks reasonably but tires or is breathless: rollator with a seat
  • Cognitive impairment or unreliable judgement: avoid rollators — the brake requirement is the problem
  • Narrow doorways at home: measure first; some rollators are wide
  • Mostly outdoor use on uneven ground: larger wheels roll far better than small castors

If a physiotherapist is involved in the patient’s care, ask them. Gait assessment is their expertise and it takes them two minutes.

Setting the height — the part that matters most

This is not optional fine-tuning. Wrong height is a direct fall risk.

  1. Have the person stand upright in the shoes they normally wear
  2. Let their arms hang relaxed at their sides
  3. The handgrips should sit level with the crease of the wrist
  4. When gripping, the elbows should bend roughly 15–20 degrees

Too high and the shoulders lift, the user cannot push down effectively, and shoulder pain follows. Too low and the user stoops forward, which shifts the centre of gravity ahead of the feet — precisely the posture that causes falls.

Re-check the height if the person changes footwear habits, and after any significant change in posture or condition.

Details worth checking before buying

  • Weight capacity — stated on the frame; match it honestly
  • Frame weight — if the user must lift it with every step, a heavy frame is unusable
  • Grip material and diameter — larger, softer grips help arthritic hands considerably
  • Rubber tips — inspect them; worn tips lose grip and are the cheapest safety item to replace
  • Brake type on rollators — loop brakes should be reachable and squeezable with the user’s actual hand strength. Test in the shop.
  • Folding — try folding it; if a caregiver cannot manage it one-handed, it will stay in the car
  • Seat height on rollators — the user must be able to rise from it unaided

Using it safely at home

  • Remove loose rugs and trailing cables along the walking route
  • Keep the walker within reach of the bed and chair, not across the room
  • Never use a walker to pull yourself up from sitting — push up from the chair arms first, then take the walker
  • Do not hang heavy bags on the handles; it tips the frame
  • Check the rubber tips and wheels monthly

Try it before buying

Walking aids should be fitted, not ordered. Bring the person to the shop if at all possible — five minutes of walking with two options settles the question faster than any specification sheet.
