Patient lifts and transfer aids: moving an elderly patient without injury

The caregiver is the most important piece of equipment in the room, and the one most often damaged. Transfer aids exist to protect them.

SMBy Saji V MathewManaging Director, Cosmic Surgicals · 16 September 2026 · 4 min read

Most home care for the elderly does not end because the patient gets worse. It ends because the caregiver hurts their back, usually during a transfer, and there is nobody else to do it. Transfer aids are how a family avoids that.

Match the aid to what the patient can do

Patient can…Use

Sit up and push with arms, but not standTransfer board, slide sheet

Slide sheet

A pair of low-friction sheets, or a tube, placed under the patient. With the sheet in place, one person can slide the patient up the bed or roll them for a change without lifting or dragging. Dragging a patient by the armpits or heels tears skin, causes shearing injuries that become pressure sores, and wrecks the caregiver’s back.

This is the first transfer aid every bedridden-care household should own. It costs very little and is used more than anything else on this page.

Transfer belt (gait belt)

A wide, padded belt fastened around the patient’s waist with handles the caregiver holds. It gives a secure grip for sit-to-stand, walking short distances, and lowering safely into a chair or onto a commode. It is for patients who can bear some weight; it does not lift them.

Never lift a patient by their arms or clothing — dislocated shoulders and torn skin are common results. The belt gives you something safe to hold.

Transfer board

A rigid, smooth board that bridges bed and wheelchair, or wheelchair and commode. The patient sits, the board goes under one thigh, and they slide across in stages. It needs some upper-body strength, similar heights on both surfaces, and armrests that swing away. Right for stroke patients and amputees with good arm strength.

Standing hoist (sit-to-stand lift)

For patients who can bear a little weight and hold on, but cannot stand unaided. A sling under the arms and a padded knee block; the machine raises them to standing and wheels them to the next seat. Preserves some leg strength and dignity, and makes toileting far easier.

Full patient hoist

For patients who cannot help at all. A sling is placed under them in bed, attached to the hoist arm, and the machine lifts them clear — to a wheelchair, commode, or shower chair — with no lifting by the caregiver.

  • Manual (hydraulic) hoists are pumped by hand; lower cost, no charging
  • Electric hoists lift at the press of a button; easier for an elderly caregiver
  • Sling choice matters — full-body, toileting, or bathing slings for different tasks, sized to the patient
  • Check the base spreads wide enough to go around a wheelchair or commode, and fits under the bed
  • Floor and doorway — measure before ordering; hoists need turning space

The bed is a transfer aid too

A height-adjustable bed is the aid people forget. Raised for care, lowered to match the wheelchair for transfers, lowered further for a patient at risk of rolling out. Combined with a slide sheet, it removes most of the bending and lifting from daily care. See our hospital bed guide.

Safe transfer basics

  1. Explain what you are about to do; let the patient help as much as they can
  2. Brakes on — bed, wheelchair, commode, hoist — every single time
  3. Match heights before sliding or boarding
  4. Feet apart, knees bent, back straight; move with your legs
  5. Never twist while holding the patient
  6. Two people for the first transfers with any new aid
