Commode chairs and bedside toileting

Toileting is the single hardest daily problem in home care, and the one families are most reluctant to ask about. A commode chair solves most of it.

SMBy Saji V MathewManaging Director, Cosmic Surgicals · 9 September 2026 · 4 min read

Families setting up home care ask about beds, wheelchairs and oxygen. They rarely ask about toileting, which is the problem that will actually dominate the daily routine and cause the most falls.

A commode chair is inexpensive and solves most of it. Here is how to choose one.

The types

Fixed bedside commode

A sturdy chair with a seat opening and a removable pan underneath. Placed beside the bed. The most stable option, and adequate wherever the patient can transfer the short distance from bed to chair with or without help.

Folding commode

The same idea in a frame that folds flat. Useful in small rooms, or where the chair needs to be stored out of sight between uses. Slightly less rigid — check the locking mechanism holds firm under the user’s full weight.

Commode wheelchair

Wheels, footrests and a commode seat combined. Lets a caregiver wheel the patient to a bathroom without a second transfer, or position the chair over an existing toilet. For patients who cannot walk at all, this is often the single most useful item in the house.

Shower commode

Built for wet use: rust-resistant frame, drainage in the seat, non-slip feet. Allows toileting and showering in one seated position. If the chair will ever go into a wet bathroom, buy this rather than adapting a dry-use commode — corroded frames fail without warning.

Raised toilet seat with frame

Not a commode chair, but worth knowing about. Where the patient can still reach the bathroom, raising the existing toilet and adding grab arms may be all that is needed, at a fraction of the cost.

Getting the fit right

  • Seat height: feet flat on the floor, knees at roughly 90 degrees. Height-adjustable legs matter, because the right height differs by person and by the bed they transfer from.
  • Seat width: comfortable but not so wide that the armrests are out of reach — the user pushes on those to stand.
  • Armrests: essential. They carry most of the load during sit-to-stand. Removable or drop-down arms make side transfers much easier.
  • Backrest: a proper backrest, not a token bar, for anyone who needs to sit for a while.
  • Weight capacity: stated and matched honestly. Bariatric models exist.
  • Non-slip feet: check them, and replace when worn.

Placement matters as much as the chair

Position the commode on the patient’s stronger side, close enough that the transfer is one step rather than several, and where the caregiver has room to stand and assist. Keep the route clear and lit — most night-time falls in home care happen on the way to toileting.

If the patient gets up at night, a night light along that route is one of the cheapest safety interventions available.

The hygiene routine

  • Empty and rinse the pan after each use
  • Use a pan with a lid to control odour between emptying
  • Clean the seat daily with a suitable disinfectant
  • Commode liners, where available, considerably reduce the unpleasantness of the routine
  • Keep gloves, wipes, a covered bin and hand sanitiser within arm’s reach of the chair
  • Check the frame and feet monthly for corrosion or wear

A word about dignity

Toileting is where home care most often becomes distressing for the patient. Small things help: a screen or curtain, keeping the routine unhurried, positioning the chair so the patient is not facing the door, and letting them do whatever part of it they still can. Equipment that makes the transfer easy is what buys the time for that.
