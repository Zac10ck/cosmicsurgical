Elderly care · Dementia

Equipment for caring for a dementia patient at home

Dementia changes what 'safe' means. Equipment that helps most elderly patients — bed rails, a bathroom lock — can harm a confused one. Here is what changes.

SMBy Saji V MathewManaging Director, Cosmic Surgicals · 16 September 2026 · 4 min read

Caring for a parent with dementia or Alzheimer’s at home is different from caring for a frail elderly parent who is mentally sharp. The person may not remember that they cannot walk unaided, may get up at 2 AM convinced it is morning, may not recognise the bathroom, and may try to climb over anything put in their way.

That changes what safe equipment looks like. Some standard elderly-care items become risks.

Bed: low, not railed

For most elderly patients, bed rails are protective. For a confused patient they can be dangerous — the person climbs over and falls from higher up. Safer approaches:

  • Low bed — an adjustable bed lowered to its minimum at night, so a roll-out is a short drop
  • Floor mat beside the bed to cushion a fall
  • Bed-exit alarm — a pressure mat under the sheet or beside the bed that alerts the caregiver when the person gets up
  • Single assist handle rather than full-length rails, for something to hold without creating a barrier

Ask the treating doctor before using rails with a dementia patient.

Night-time wandering

Getting up at night is when most dementia-related falls and wandering happen.

  • Bed-exit or floor sensor alarm so the caregiver knows the moment the person is up
  • Night lighting along the route to the bathroom, on all night
  • Bedside commode — removes the reason to walk anywhere
  • Secure external doors — a lock the person cannot operate, or a door alarm. Never lock the person in a room; the caregiver must be able to reach them in seconds
  • Clear the route of anything to trip on or knock over

Bathroom: simple and visible

  • Shower chair, grab bars, non-slip mats — as for any elderly person
  • Raised toilet seat in a contrasting colour — a white seat on a white toilet in a white bathroom can be invisible to someone with dementia
  • No lock the person can use alone; a door that opens from outside
  • Remove or secure razors, medicines, cleaning products, loose electrical items
  • Thermostatic or clearly marked taps to prevent scalding
  • Keep the layout unchanged — moving things is disorienting

See our bathroom safety guide for the equipment itself.

Mobility aids with a caution

A walker or stick only helps if the person remembers to use it. Many dementia patients leave the walker beside the chair and walk without it. Options:

  • Keep the aid in the person’s hands or directly in front of them, not beside
  • Prefer a chair with arms at the right height over a walker for short distances
  • Rollators with brakes are usually unsuitable — the brake step is forgotten
  • Grab bars along regularly walked routes work better than carried aids

Medication and monitoring

  • Locked medicine storage — the person may take doses repeatedly or not at all
  • Pill organiser managed by the caregiver, not the patient
  • Large-display BP monitor and thermometer for observations
  • A simple notebook for behaviour, sleep, food and observations — patterns matter to the doctor

Reducing confusion

  • Large clock and calendar visible from the bed
  • Consistent furniture placement; do not rearrange
  • Contrasting colours: dark toilet seat, coloured tape on step edges, plates that contrast with the table
  • Remove mirrors if reflections cause distress
  • Reduce noise and clutter

The caregiver

Dementia care is exhausting in a way that is hard to explain to anyone who has not done it — the vigilance never stops. A bed-exit alarm is as much for the caregiver’s sleep as for the patient’s safety. A commode beside the bed means the caregiver is not walking someone to the bathroom four times a night. Buy the equipment that lets the caregiver rest; it is what keeps the arrangement going.
