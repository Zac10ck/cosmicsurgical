Buying a hospital bed for home care in Kerala

A hospital bed is usually the single biggest home-care purchase a family makes. Getting the function count, the mattress and the room access right matters more than the brand.

SMBy Saji V MathewManaging Director, Cosmic Surgicals · 9 September 2026 · 5 min read

When a family member is discharged with a long recovery ahead, or a palliative patient moves to home care, the hospital bed is usually the first and biggest purchase. It is also the one people most often get slightly wrong — not by choosing a bad bed, but by choosing the wrong configuration for the room and the caregiver.

Start with the caregiver, not the patient

This sounds backwards, but it is the most useful reframing we can offer. The patient will be in the bed. The caregiver will be operating it — several times a day, possibly for months, possibly alone, possibly while also managing a household. If the caregiver is a 65-year-old spouse, a manual crank bed that needs adjusting eight times a day is a poor decision regardless of price.

Electric vs manual

Manual (fowler and semi-fowler)

Crank-operated. Lower cost, nothing to fail electrically, no dependence on power supply. A semi-fowler bed adjusts the backrest; a full fowler adjusts backrest and knee-rest. Good for patients who need occasional repositioning and have an able caregiver.

Electric

Motorised, hand-control operated. Typically sold as two-function (backrest, knee-rest), three-function (adds height adjustment), or five-function (adds trendelenburg tilts). Height adjustment is the underrated one — it saves the caregiver’s back during transfers, sponge baths and dressing changes.

Our practical rule: if the patient will be repositioned more than three or four times a day, buy electric. If power cuts are a concern, ask about models with a battery backup.

How many functions do you actually need?

  • Two-function: Enough for most recovery and general home-care cases.
  • Three-function: Add height adjustment where the caregiver does frequent transfers or personal care. This is the sweet spot for most families.
  • Five-function: Tilt positions, usually needed only where a clinician has specifically advised them.

Paying for five functions and using two is common. Ask the treating doctor or physiotherapist what the patient actually requires.

Measure before you order

The single most frequent problem we see is a bed that will not reach the room. Before ordering, measure:

  • Doorway width — including any turn immediately outside the door
  • Corridor turns — a long frame needs swing room
  • Lift dimensions if the flat is above ground floor
  • Room space — allow clearance on one long side for the caregiver, ideally both
  • Power point location for an electric bed

Most beds can be delivered dismantled and assembled in the room, but confirm this rather than assume it.

The mattress is not an afterthought

The mattress does more for patient comfort and skin safety than the frame does. Options:

  • Standard foam mattress — fine for short recovery where the patient still moves and gets up.
  • Air / alternating pressure mattress — for patients in bed most of the day. The pump alternates pressure across cells so no single area of skin is loaded continuously. If the patient is bedridden, treat this as essential rather than optional. We cover it in detail in our bed sore prevention guide.
  • Water bed / gel overlay — an alternative pressure-distribution approach in some cases.

Match the mattress to the bed platform dimensions. A mattress that is slightly too small leaves gaps at the side rails, which is a genuine safety issue.

Accessories worth buying at the same time

  • Side rails — for patients at risk of rolling out, or who use them to reposition themselves
  • Overbed table — meals, medicines, reading; makes the patient far more independent
  • IV stand — if home infusions or feeds are expected
  • Bedside locker — keeps medication and supplies within the caregiver’s reach
  • Castors with brakes — check the bed has them and that they lock properly

Buying accessories with the bed means they are guaranteed compatible. Sourcing them later often means they are not.

Questions to ask the shop

  1. What is the safe working load, and what is the patient’s weight?
  2. Is the frame steel or aluminium, and what is the finish?
  3. Which motor is fitted, and what is the warranty on it specifically?
  4. Is there battery backup for power cuts?
  5. Is delivery, assembly and demonstration included in the price?
  6. Who services it locally, and how quickly can spare parts be sourced?
