Choosing a patient monitor for a clinic or ward

Buy the parameters your staff will actually act on. A monitor displaying data nobody responds to trains people to ignore alarms.

SMBy Saji V MathewManaging Director, Cosmic Surgicals · 9 September 2026 · 4 min read

Patient monitors are bought on screen size and parameter count, and lived with through their alarms and consumables. The second set of factors matters far more.

Decide the parameters first

NIBP — non-invasive blood pressure

Present on essentially every monitor. The thing to check is cuff sizes: you need the sizes your actual patient population requires, including paediatric or large-adult cuffs. A wrong-sized cuff gives a wrong reading, exactly as with a home BP monitor.

SpO2 — oxygen saturation

Standard and genuinely useful everywhere. Check which probe types are supplied and whether paediatric and disposable probes are available.

ECG

Continuous rhythm display. Necessary for procedure rooms, recovery and wards where rhythm matters. Check lead configurations supported (3-lead, 5-lead) and that electrodes are easily sourced.

Respiration

Usually derived from ECG impedance. Useful, but be aware it is an estimate and is prone to artefact from movement.

Temperature

Continuous monitoring via probe. Add it where clinical practice requires continuous temperature — otherwise intermittent measurement is adequate and cheaper.

Capnography (EtCO2)

End-tidal CO2. Important in sedation, anaesthesia and ventilated patients, and a significant cost addition. Specify it only where practice requires it.

The discipline here: buy the parameters your staff will act on. A monitor displaying data nobody responds to adds alarms, cost and noise, and it teaches staff that alarms can be ignored.

Alarms are the feature that matters most

Alarm fatigue is a real and well-documented safety problem: when most alerts are false, staff stop responding to all of them. When evaluating a monitor, look for:

  • Configurable limits per patient, not just global defaults
  • Clear priority tiers — visually and audibly distinct high, medium and low priority
  • Adjustable alarm volume with a sensible floor
  • Alarm history for review
  • Sensible artefact rejection — how does it behave when a probe falls off?

Then actually configure them. Factory default limits set for an adult ICU patient will alarm constantly on a paediatric patient, and vice versa.

Practical evaluation points

  • Display legibility from across the room — the distance staff will actually read it from, at the angle they will read it
  • Battery runtime under realistic use, and local battery replacement availability
  • Mounting — trolley, wall bracket, or bed rail; confirm it is included or quoted
  • Data output — printing, USB export, or network connectivity if you need records
  • Cleaning — sealed surfaces that tolerate your disinfection protocol
  • Cable management — a genuine daily irritation on badly designed units

Consumables: the real total cost

Over a monitor’s working life, consumables frequently exceed the purchase price:

  • NIBP cuffs in every size used, replaced as they wear
  • SpO2 probes — reusable and disposable
  • ECG electrodes and lead sets
  • Temperature probes and covers
  • Capnography sampling lines, where fitted

Ask for a consumables price list at the quotation stage and confirm local stocking. A monitor whose probes take three weeks to arrive is a monitor that sits idle.

Service and calibration

Confirm before purchase: the service interval, who performs it locally, whether calibration is included, and the spare-part lead time. For a device that clinical decisions rest on, this is not a detail to settle after delivery.

A sensible specification for most clinics

For a general clinic or small ward: a five-parameter monitor (NIBP, SpO2, ECG, respiration, temperature), cuffs in the sizes you actually use, reusable plus a stock of disposable SpO2 probes, battery backup, trolley mount, and a configured alarm profile per patient type.
