Oxygen concentrator buying guide for home use

An oxygen concentrator is prescribed equipment, not a general-purpose appliance. The flow rate must match the prescription — here is how to read the specification sheet.

Published 9 September 2026 by Cosmic Surgicals, Kottayam · Updated for 2026

Of all the equipment families buy for home care, the oxygen concentrator is the one where getting the specification wrong has the most direct consequences. It is also the one most often bought in a hurry, at the point of hospital discharge, with no time to research.

This guide is what we would tell you across the counter.

First: get the prescription details

Before looking at any machine, you need three numbers from the treating doctor:

  • Prescribed flow rate in litres per minute (LPM)
  • Hours of use per day — continuous, nocturnal only, or as needed
  • Target oxygen saturation the therapy is aiming to maintain

Everything else follows from these. A shop that sells you a concentrator without asking for the prescribed flow rate is not doing you a favour.

Understanding capacity: 5 LPM vs 10 LPM

The headline number is the maximum continuous flow the unit can deliver. The important detail is that oxygen purity falls as flow increases on most machines. A 5 LPM concentrator may deliver 93% ± 3% oxygen at 5 litres but only its rated purity at lower settings — check the specification table, not just the headline.

Practical guidance:

  • Up to 5 LPM prescribed: a 5 LPM home unit is normally appropriate
  • Above 5 LPM, or two patients / two outlets: a 8–10 LPM unit
  • Mobility required: a portable concentrator, but note these are usually pulse-dose rather than continuous flow

Continuous flow vs pulse dose

A continuous flow machine delivers oxygen steadily whether the patient is inhaling or not. This is what most home prescriptions require, and it is what you need for use with CPAP/BiPAP or during sleep.

A pulse dose machine delivers a burst when it detects inhalation. It is efficient and allows small, portable, battery-powered units — but it is not suitable for every patient, particularly during sleep when breathing is shallow. Do not substitute one for the other without asking the doctor.

Purity, and why the alarm matters

Home concentrators typically produce 90–96% oxygen. Look for a unit with an oxygen concentration status indicator or low-purity alarm. Sieve beds degrade over time, and without an indicator you have no way of knowing the machine has quietly stopped delivering therapeutic oxygen.

Practical factors people forget

Noise

Rated in decibels, typically 40–55 dB. If the machine will run beside a patient’s bed all night, in a small room, the difference between 42 dB and 55 dB is the difference between sleeping and not sleeping. Ask to hear it running.

Heat and ventilation

Concentrators generate heat and need clear air space around the intake — usually 30 cm or more on all sides. In a small Kerala bedroom in April, this matters. Plan where it will physically sit.

Running cost

At 300–600 W running 24 hours a day, electricity is a real ongoing cost. Get the wattage figure at the prescribed flow setting and calculate it before you buy.

Power cuts

Concentrators do not store oxygen. If the patient is oxygen-dependent, discuss a backup plan with the doctor — typically a small cylinder kept for emergencies — and make sure the household knows where it is.

Maintenance you will actually have to do

  • Gross particle filter: rinse and dry, often weekly
  • Cabinet filter: replace per manufacturer schedule
  • Humidifier bottle: clean and refill daily with distilled or boiled cooled water if humidification is used
  • Cannula and tubing: replace regularly; cannulas are consumables, not permanent
  • Professional service: at the manufacturer’s stated interval

Ask the shop to demonstrate filter cleaning before the machine leaves the counter, and ask who performs the professional service locally.

Before you pay, confirm

  1. Maximum flow and purity at your prescribed setting
  2. Continuous flow or pulse dose
  3. Noise level in dB
  4. Power consumption in watts
  5. Warranty length, and whether it covers the compressor and sieve beds
  6. Local service availability and spare-part lead time
  7. Whether a demonstration and setup at home is included
