Preventing falls at home: equipment that actually works for the elderly

A hip fracture at eighty changes everything for a family. Almost every fall we hear about happened on a route that a few hundred rupees of equipment would have made safe.

SMBy Saji V MathewManaging Director, Cosmic Surgicals · 16 September 2026 · 4 min read

Falls are the leading cause of injury and loss of independence in old age. A hip fracture at eighty frequently means surgery, months of bed rest, and a family suddenly needing everything on our bedridden care guide. Almost every fall we hear about at the shop happened in one of three places, and could have been prevented with equipment that costs less than one hospital day.

Where falls happen

  1. The bathroom — wet floor, nothing to hold, a low toilet to rise from
  2. Bed to bathroom at night — dark, hurried, dizzy from standing quickly
  3. The first steps after standing — blood pressure drops, legs not yet steady

Fix those three routes first. Everything else is secondary.

Bathroom

  • Grab bars beside the toilet and in the shower, screwed into solid wall. Not suction bars — those fail without warning on Kerala’s damp tiles.
  • Raised toilet seat with arms — reduces the depth of the sit-to-stand, which is where the effort and the fall risk is
  • Shower chair — bathe seated. Standing in a wet shower is the single riskiest daily activity.
  • Non-slip mats inside and outside the shower, and rubber-soled footwear
  • Hand-held shower so the person is not turning under a fixed head

Our bathroom safety guide goes into each item.

Bedroom and the night route

  • Bed rail or bed assist handle — something to hold when sitting up and standing
  • Bed at the right height — feet flat on the floor when sitting on the edge, knees at 90°. Too high or too low is a fall at every transfer.
  • Night lights along the route to the bathroom, switched on automatically or left on
  • Bedside commode if the bathroom is far — removes the night walk entirely
  • Walking aid within reach of the bed, on the side the person gets up from
  • Clear the route — rugs, cables, furniture edges, a threshold step

Standing up safely

Many falls happen in the ten seconds after standing, when blood pressure drops. Teach the routine: sit on the edge, count to ten, then stand. Equipment that helps:

  • Chair with arms at a height the person can rise from — low soft sofas are a fall waiting to happen
  • Chair raisers or a high-seat chair
  • Walking stick, quad stick or walker, correctly fitted — see our walking aid guide

Protecting against the fall you cannot prevent

  • Hip protectors — padded undergarments that reduce hip fracture risk on impact. Useful for frail people who fall repeatedly and in care homes.
  • Low bed or floor mat beside the bed for patients who roll out
  • Personal alarm or a bell within reach, so a fallen person is not on the floor for hours

The house itself

  • Good lighting everywhere the person walks, with switches reachable from the bed and the doorway
  • Remove loose rugs, or tape them down
  • Mark step edges and thresholds with contrasting tape
  • Handrails on both sides of stairs; a ramp for the main entrance step
  • Keep frequently used items at waist height, not on high shelves or the floor

Start with a walk-through

Walk the routes the person actually uses — bed to bathroom, chair to kitchen, door to gate — and note where they steady themselves against a wall or furniture. Those are the spots that need a bar or a rail. It takes ten minutes and finds the risks a catalogue never will.

Fall prevention — FAQs

The bathroom, the route between bed and bathroom at night, and the first few steps after standing up. Those three places should get the first attention: grab bars and non-slip surfaces in the bathroom, a lit and clear path from the bed, and a walking aid within reach of the bed and chair.

Used correctly, yes — they give something to hold while repositioning and prevent rolling out. They should not be used to confine a confused patient who will try to climb over them, which causes worse falls. Ask the treating clinician if the patient has dementia.

Hip protectors are padded garments that absorb impact over the hip bone. They reduce the risk of hip fracture in a fall for people who wear them consistently, particularly in care homes and for frail patients who fall repeatedly. They do not prevent the fall itself.

There is no single height — it depends on the user and the movement. Bars beside a toilet are typically fitted so the user's forearm is roughly horizontal when gripping while seated; shower bars are placed for standing and for lowering onto a shower chair. Fit them into solid wall, never plasterboard or tiles alone.
