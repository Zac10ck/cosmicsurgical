Imported vs Indian medical equipment: which should you buy?

“Is it imported?” is the most common question at our counter. It is also the wrong first question. Here is what to ask instead.

SMBy Saji V MathewManaging Director, Cosmic Surgicals · 9 September 2026 · 4 min read

“Is it imported?” is the question we hear most often across the counter. Behind it is a reasonable instinct — buyers want a proxy for quality, and country of origin feels like one.

It is a weak proxy. Here is a better way to think about it.

What origin actually tells you

Very little on its own. There are excellent Indian manufacturers producing hospital beds, furniture and instruments that outlast cheaper imports by years. There are also imported products at every quality level, including the bottom. The label tells you where it was assembled, not how well.

What varies more reliably by origin is which categories are strong. Broadly, and with plenty of exceptions:

  • Indian manufacturing is generally strong in hospital furniture, bed frames, trolleys, stainless steel instruments and general consumables — fabrication-heavy items where local production is mature and spare parts are easy.
  • Imports often lead in precision electronics, advanced diagnostic and monitoring devices, and some specialised motorised equipment.

The five comparisons that actually matter

1. Build materials and finish

For a bed or trolley: gauge of the steel, quality of the welds, powder-coat finish, castor quality. You can assess most of this by hand in the shop. Do.

2. The motor or moving part

On an electric bed or powered chair, the motor is what fails. Ask which motor is fitted and what its warranty is separately from the frame warranty.

3. Warranty terms in writing

Not the length — the coverage. What is included, what is excluded, is labour covered, and who honours it. An imported product with a two-year warranty that must be claimed through a distributor in another state is worth less than a one-year warranty serviced locally.

4. Spare-part availability and lead time

This is where imports most often disappoint. Ask directly: if this part fails in month fourteen, how long until I have a replacement? For equipment a patient depends on daily, a six-week wait is not acceptable at any price.

5. Local service

Who physically repairs it, and where are they? A product nobody within 200 km can service is a liability regardless of quality.

The total cost view

Purchase price is one line in a longer sum. Over five years the real cost includes servicing, consumables, spare parts and — for clinical equipment — the cost of downtime. A cheaper unit that is out of service for a month a year is not cheaper.

For low-value consumables, buy on price. For anything motorised, precision, or patient-critical, buy on total cost and service support.

Certification and documentation

Regardless of origin, ask for the documentation that applies to the specific product: manufacturer details, model number, country of origin, applicable certifications, and for regulated devices the importer or manufacturer licence reference. A supplier who will put this on the quotation is one you can hold to it.

How to run the comparison

Ask your supplier to quote one imported and one Indian option for the same requirement, with:

  1. Brand, model number, country of origin
  2. Key specifications relevant to your use — load rating, functions, capacity
  3. Warranty length and exactly what it covers
  4. Spare-part lead time
  5. Local service arrangement
  6. Delivery and installation, included or extra

Laid out side by side, the right answer is usually obvious — and it is not always the more expensive one.
